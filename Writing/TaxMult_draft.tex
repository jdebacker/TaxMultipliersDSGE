\begin{abstract}
This paper analyzes the stabilization properties of tax cuts in a DSGE model.  We do this by simulating the a general equilibrium model with a rich set of tax levers in order to quantify tax multipliers over the short and medium-term.  The novelty in this paper is that we focus on tax cuts that directly target consumption and investment, which are the most effective counter-cyclical fiscal policies.  We find that the short-term multiplier associated with a cut to the consumption tax is larger than the multiplier associated with a cut to the labor income tax, and that the short-term multipliers associated with cuts to investment taxes are even larger.  We compare these to the multipliers associated with cuts to broad taxes on capital and labor income, which are the focus of much of the existing literature.  We also find that the medium-term multipliers associated with cuts to consumption and investment taxes are larger than the medium-term multipliers associated with cuts to broad taxes on capital and labor income.  These results suggest that tax cuts that directly target consumption and investment are more effective counter-cyclical fiscal policies than tax cuts that target broad taxes on capital and labor income.
\end{abstract}






%\section{Introduction}
%\label{sec:intro}


We study tax multipliers associated with taxes that directly stimulate private consumption spending or investment such as consumption taxes and investment tax credits.  Consumption taxes have long been favored by tax economists for their relative efficiency vis-a-vis taxes on capital and labor.  This paper attempts to argue that consumption taxes should also be favored by macroeconomists for their stabilization properties.  Cuts to consumption taxes can quickly and effectively stimulate aggregate demand. The following analysis studies the quantitative impact of tax cuts in a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) model.  In addition to consumption taxes, the model nests other tax policies, such as investment tax credits and changes to expensing and depreciation policies.  These policies target investment by lowering the after-tax cost of capital.  Historically, such instruments have been used by the U.S. to provide counter-cyclical fiscal policy (e.g. the Economic Recovery Act of 1981 increased the ability of businesses to take advantage of the investment tax credit).  Despite their use, such policies have not been studied in a DSGE environment.

The goal of this paper is to study specific tax policies in a DSGE environment.  A number of researchers have used DSGE models to study the government spending multiplier (e.g., \cn{CER2010}), but less work has been done on tax multipliers.  Those who provide insights on tax multipliers (e.g., \cn{Zubairy2010}, \cn{IMF2010}) focus on multipliers associated with broad taxes on capital and labor income.  Such tax cuts are generally not the most effective counter-cyclical policies.  We are the first to provide quantitative insights into the the size of the multipliers associated with tax cuts that directly target consumption and investment.

Some related work to mention:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \cn{IMF2010} shows impulse response functions to a consumption tax cut.  This paper seems to find that consumption taxes do well compared to other taxes and transfers, but don't do as well as gov't consumption and gov't investment
\item \cn{LPT2010} have consumption taxes, but don't allow them to vary with gov't debt
\item \cn{Zubairy2010} doesn't not have consumption taxes, only capital and labor income taxes.  Says few even have distortionary taxes in models of multipliers.
\item \cn{TY2010} allow for consumption taxes and allow them to vary.  Not answering the same question as I want, but good to look at what they say about consumption taxes.  In particular, how with cons taxes the consumer and producer price index will vary and what crowding out happens with consumption tax changes.
\item \cn{SC2005} have consumption taxes, but don't use them as a policy instrument.  They don't say anything interesting about consumption taxes.
\item \cn{CER2010} don't have consumption taxes and only focus on spending multipliers.  I don't even think they have taxes.  Fiscal and monetary policy rules look simple though- might want to take these.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Households}
The economy is populated by a continuum of identical households. Each household maximizes expected lifetime utility over its habit-adjusted private consumption surplus $x_{c,t}$, hours worked $h_t$, and habit-adjusted government consumption surplus $x_{g,t}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
E_{0}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t} d_t \left\{ \frac{\left[x_{c,t}^{a}(1-h_{t})^{1-a}\right]^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma} + V(x_{g,t}) \right\}
\end{equation}
where $\beta \in (0,1)$ is the discount factor, $d_t$ is an intertemporal preference shock, $\gamma$ is the coefficient of relative risk aversion, and $a \in (0,1)$ is the consumption expenditure share calibrated at the steady state. Utility over government consumption $V(x_{g,t})$ is separable from private utility, so government spending does not directly affect the marginal utility of private consumption or leisure. Following \cn{Zubairy2010}, the model incorporates deep habit formation in both private and government consumption as in \cn{RSU2006}, which generates countercyclical markups and allows private consumption to rise in response to a government spending shock.

The habit-adjusted consumption surpluses are:
\begin{align}
x_{c,t} &= c_t - b_c s_{C,t-1} \label{eq:xc} \\
x_{g,t} &= g_t - b_g s_{G,t-1} \label{eq:xg}
\end{align}
where $b_c \in [0,1)$ and $b_g \in [0,1)$ govern the degree of deep habit formation in private and government consumption. The habit stocks evolve according to:
\begin{align}
s_{C,t} &= \theta^c s_{C,t-1} + (1-\theta^c) c_t \label{eq:sC} \\
s_{G,t} &= \theta^g s_{G,t-1} + (1-\theta^g) g_t \label{eq:sG}
\end{align}
where $\theta^c, \theta^g \in [0,1)$ govern the speed of habit stock adjustment.

Households supply labor, accumulate physical capital, hold government bonds, and receive dividend income from firms. The period-by-period real budget constraint is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bc}
\begin{split}
(1+\tau_{t}^{c})c_{t} + b_{t} + i_{t} = &\;\frac{\left[1+(R_{t-1}-1)(1-\tau_{t}^{i})\right]b_{t-1}}{\pi_t} + (1-\tau_{t}^{w})w_{t}h_{t} \\
&+ (1-\tau_{t}^{k})r_{t}^{k}u_{t}k_{t-1} + \tau_{t}^{k}\delta^{\tau}k_{t-1}^{\tau} + \tau_{t}^{k}e_{t}^{\tau}i_{t} + \tau_{t}^{ic}i_{t} + (1-\tau_{t}^{d})\,\text{div}_{t} + x_{t}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $b_t$ denotes real bond holdings, $R_t$ is the gross nominal interest rate, $\pi_t \equiv P_t/P_{t-1}$ is gross price inflation, $w_t$ is the real wage, $r_t^k$ is the real rental rate of capital, $u_t$ is the capital utilization rate, $\text{div}_t$ are real firm dividends, and $x_t$ is a lump-sum government transfer. The tax instruments are: $\tau_t^c$ (consumption tax), $\tau_t^w$ (labor income tax), $\tau_t^k$ (capital income tax), $\tau_t^i$ (interest income tax), $\tau_t^d$ (dividend income tax), and $\tau_t^{ic}$ (investment tax credit). Only the interest component $(R_{t-1}-1)$ of the gross bond return is subject to the interest income tax. Capital income is measured net of tax depreciation deductions $\tau_t^k\delta^{\tau}k_{t-1}^{\tau}$, where $\delta^{\tau}$ is the tax depreciation rate and $k_t^{\tau}$ is the tax basis of the capital stock, and net of immediate expensing $\tau_t^k e_t^{\tau} i_t$, where $e_t^{\tau}$ is the expensing rate.

Physical capital accumulates according to:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kacc}
k_{t} = (1-\delta)k_{t-1} + i_{t}\left[1-S\!\left(\frac{\mu_t i_{t}}{i_{t-1}}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the constant economic depreciation rate and $\mu_t$ is a stochastic investment efficiency shock. Following \cn{SW2003} and \cn{Zubairy2010}, the investment adjustment cost function takes the form $S\!\left(\frac{\mu_t i_t}{i_{t-1}}\right) = \frac{\kappa}{2}\!\left(\frac{\mu_t i_t}{i_{t-1}}-1\right)^2$, so that in the deterministic steady state $S = S' = 0$ and $S'' = \kappa > 0$.

The tax basis of the capital stock evolves as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ktau}
k_{t}^{\tau} = (1-\delta^{\tau})k_{t-1}^{\tau} + i_{t}(1-e_{t}^{\tau})
\end{equation}
Only the non-expensed fraction $(1-e_t^{\tau})$ of new investment is added to the tax basis; the immediately expensed portion is deducted in the period of investment.

\subsubsection*{Household optimality conditions}

The first-order condition with respect to consumption defines the marginal utility of income $\lambda_t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lambda}
\lambda_t = \frac{d_t \cdot a \cdot \left[x_{c,t}^{a}(1-h_t)^{1-a}\right]^{1-\gamma}}{x_{c,t}(1+\tau_{t}^{c})}
\end{equation}
The consumption tax $\tau_t^c$ drives a wedge between the marginal utility of income and the marginal utility of the consumption surplus: a higher $\tau_t^c$ lowers $\lambda_t$ for any given level of $x_{c,t}$ and $h_t$.

The intratemporal labor-leisure condition equates the marginal disutility of work to the after-markup real wage:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:labor}
d_t (1-a) \frac{\left[x_{c,t}^{a}(1-h_t)^{1-a}\right]^{1-\gamma}}{1-h_t} = \frac{\lambda_t w_t}{\tilde{w}_t}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{w}_t$ is the wage markup arising from monopolistic competition in the labor market.

The optimal utilization rate equates the after-tax marginal product of capital to the marginal cost of utilization:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:util}
(1-\tau_{t}^{k})r_{t}^{k} = \gamma_1 u_{t}^{\sigma_u}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_1 = (1-\tau_k^{ss})r_k^{ss}$ is pinned down at the steady state and $\sigma_u$ governs the convexity of the utilization cost function $a(u_t) = \frac{\gamma_1}{1+\sigma_u}(u_t^{1+\sigma_u}-1)$. Relative to \cn{Zubairy2010}, the term $\delta q_t \tau_t^k$ is absent from this condition because depreciation deductions are now tracked through the shadow value of the tax basis $v_{\tau,t}$ defined below, avoiding double-counting.

The capital Euler equation governs the optimal capital stock:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:capeuler}
\lambda_t q_t = \beta E_t \lambda_{t+1} \left[(1-\tau_{t+1}^{k})r_{t+1}^{k}u_{t+1} - \frac{\gamma_1}{1+\sigma_u}\!\left(u_{t+1}^{1+\sigma_u}-1\right) + q_{t+1}(1-\delta)\right]
\end{equation}
where $q_t$ is Tobin's marginal $q$. Similarly, the term $\delta q_{t+1}\tau_{t+1}^k u_{t+1}$ present in \cn{Zubairy2010} is omitted here for the same reason.

The investment Euler equation governs the optimal investment decision:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inv}
\lambda_t\!\left(1-\tau_{t}^{ic}-\tau_{t}^{k}e_{t}^{\tau}\right) = \lambda_t q_t \!\left[1-S\!\left(\tfrac{\mu_t i_t}{i_{t-1}}\right) - \tfrac{\mu_t i_t}{i_{t-1}} S'\!\left(\tfrac{\mu_t i_t}{i_{t-1}}\right)\right] + \beta E_t \lambda_{t+1} q_{t+1} \!\left(\tfrac{\mu_{t+1}i_{t+1}}{i_t}\right)^{\!2} S'\!\left(\tfrac{\mu_{t+1}i_{t+1}}{i_t}\right) + \lambda_t v_{\tau,t}(1-e_{t}^{\tau})
\end{equation}
The left-hand side is the net cost of investing one unit: the investment tax credit $\tau_t^{ic}$ and the immediate expensing saving $\tau_t^k e_t^{\tau}$ each reduce the effective outlay. The term $\lambda_t v_{\tau,t}(1-e_t^{\tau})$ on the right-hand side is the present value of future tax depreciation deductions on the non-expensed share of investment. The shadow value of the tax basis $v_{\tau,t}$ satisfies its own Euler equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vtau}
\lambda_t v_{\tau,t} = \beta E_t \lambda_{t+1}\!\left[(1-\delta^{\tau})v_{\tau,t+1} + \tau_{t+1}^{k}\delta^{\tau}\right]
\end{equation}
An additional unit of tax basis yields a deduction $\tau_{t+1}^k\delta^{\tau}$ next period and a depreciated stock $(1-\delta^{\tau})v_{\tau,t+1}$ of future deductions.

Finally, the bond Euler equation is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bondeuler}
\lambda_t = \beta E_t \!\left[1+(R_t-1)(1-\tau_{t+1}^{i})\right]\frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\pi_{t+1}}
\end{equation}
Only the interest earned $(R_t-1)$ on bond holdings is subject to the interest income tax $\tau_{t+1}^i$, so the relevant return is the after-tax net interest. This differs from \cn{Zubairy2010}, in which the pre-tax gross return $R_t$ governs the bond Euler.










\subsection{Firms}

\subsubsection{Final Goods Producers}

A continuum of monopolistically competitive firms indexed by $i \in [0,1]$ each produce a differentiated variety of output demanded by households, the government, and for investment. Because households and the government form deep habits over individual varieties (Section~\ref{sec:model}), the demand for variety $i$ in the private consumption and government markets is:
\begin{align}
c_{i,t} &= \left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_t}\right)^{-\eta}x_{c,t} + b_c s_{C,i,t-1} \label{eq:cdem} \\
g_{i,t} &= \left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_t}\right)^{-\eta}x_{g,t} + b_g s_{G,i,t-1} \label{eq:gdem}
\end{align}
Investment demand for variety $i$ is $i_{i,t} = (P_{i,t}/P_t)^{-\eta}(y_t - c_t - g_t)$; there are no deep habits in investment. Here $\eta > 1$ is the elasticity of substitution across varieties and $P_t$ is the aggregate price index. The habit-inelastic components $b_c s_{C,i,t-1}$ and $b_g s_{G,i,t-1}$ make a portion of demand perfectly price-inelastic, generating countercyclical markups: when aggregate demand expands, the price-elastic share rises, increasing effective demand elasticity and compressing markups. Firms therefore invest in their customer base by holding prices low today to earn higher profits on a larger habit stock in the future.

\subsubsection{Intermediate Goods Producers}

Each firm $i$ produces output using capital services $u_t k_{i,t-1}$ and labor $h_{i,t}$ according to a Cobb-Douglas technology with a fixed operating cost $\psi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prodfunc}
\tilde{y}_{i,t} = z_t(u_t k_{i,t-1})^{\theta}h_{i,t}^{1-\theta} - \psi
\end{equation}
where $z_t$ is an aggregate technology shock, $\theta$ is the capital income share, and $\psi$ is calibrated to match a realistic profit-to-output ratio in steady state. The technology shock follows an AR(1) process:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:techshock}
\log z_t = \rho_z \log z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t^z, \quad \varepsilon_t^z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_z^2)
\end{equation}
where $\rho_z \in [0,1)$. Cost minimization at given factor prices yields the symmetric-equilibrium factor demand conditions:
\begin{align}
w_t &= mc_t \cdot z_t(1-\theta)(u_t k_{t-1})^{\theta}h_t^{-\theta} \label{eq:wfoc} \\
r_t^k &= mc_t \cdot z_t\theta(u_t k_{t-1})^{\theta-1}h_t^{1-\theta} \label{eq:rkfoc}
\end{align}
where $mc_t$ is the common real marginal cost across firms.

Following Rotemberg (1982), each firm faces a quadratic cost of changing its nominal price, which reduces output available for final demand. In symmetric equilibrium, aggregate output net of price adjustment costs is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ynet}
y_t = z_t(u_t k_{t-1})^{\theta}h_t^{1-\theta} - \psi - \frac{\alpha_P}{2}(\pi_t - \bar{\pi})^2
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_P > 0$ governs the magnitude of price adjustment costs and $\bar{\pi}$ is the steady-state gross inflation rate.

Because deep habits create dynamic pricing incentives, each firm tracks the shadow value of its customer base in each demand segment. In symmetric equilibrium, the shadow values $\tilde{\nu}_t^c$, $\tilde{\nu}_t^g$, and $\tilde{\nu}_t^i$ for the private consumption, government consumption, and investment markets satisfy:
\begin{align}
1 - mc_t - \tilde{\nu}_t^i &= 0 \label{eq:nui} \\
\frac{1 - mc_t - \tilde{\nu}_t^c}{\theta^c - 1} &= \beta E_t \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\!\left[b_c\tilde{\nu}_{t+1}^c + \frac{\theta^c}{\theta^c-1}\!\left(1-mc_{t+1}-\tilde{\nu}_{t+1}^c\right)\right] \label{eq:nuc} \\
\frac{1 - mc_t - \tilde{\nu}_t^g}{\theta^g - 1} &= \beta E_t \frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\!\left[b_g\tilde{\nu}_{t+1}^g + \frac{\theta^g}{\theta^g-1}\!\left(1-mc_{t+1}-\tilde{\nu}_{t+1}^g\right)\right] \label{eq:nug}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:nui} states that the investment market, lacking habit-inelastic demand, yields a zero effective markup. Equations~\eqref{eq:nuc}--\eqref{eq:nug} are forward-looking Euler equations: a firm accepts a lower current markup to build its habit stock, earning higher future rents.

These conditions together with the demand structure yield the aggregate price Phillips curve:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pricePC}
\eta\!\left(\tilde{\nu}_t^c x_{c,t} + \tilde{\nu}_t^g x_{g,t} + \tilde{\nu}_t^i(y_t - c_t - g_t)\right) + \alpha_P\pi_t(\pi_t - \bar{\pi}) - y_t = \alpha_P\beta E_t\frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\pi_{t+1}(\pi_{t+1}-\bar{\pi})
\end{equation}

Each household is also a monopolistic supplier of a differentiated labor variety and faces quadratic wage adjustment costs. Defining gross wage inflation $\pi_t^w \equiv (w_t / w_{t-1})\pi_t$, the wage Phillips curve is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wageinfl}
\pi_t^w = \frac{w_t}{w_{t-1}}\pi_t
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wagePC}
(\eta_w - 1)(1-\tau_t^w)h_t + \alpha_W\pi_t^w(\pi_t^w - \bar{\pi}) - \frac{\eta_w h_t}{\tilde{w}_t} = \alpha_W\beta E_t\frac{\lambda_{t+1}}{\lambda_t}\pi_{t+1}^w(\pi_{t+1}^w - \bar{\pi})
\end{equation}
where $\eta_w > 1$ is the elasticity of substitution across labor varieties and $\alpha_W$ governs wage adjustment costs. The labor income tax $\tau_t^w$ enters the wage Phillips curve because it affects the effective value of the marginal product of labor to the household.

Firm dividends are the residual of net output after paying all factors:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:div}
\text{div}_t = y_t - w_t h_t - r_t^k u_t k_{t-1}
\end{equation}

The aggregate resource constraint equates gross production (net of fixed costs) to all expenditure uses plus utilization and price and wage adjustment costs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rc}
z_t(u_t k_{t-1})^{\theta}h_t^{1-\theta} - \psi = c_t + g_t + i_t + \frac{\gamma_1}{1+\sigma_u}(u_t^{1+\sigma_u}-1)k_{t-1} + \frac{\alpha_P}{2}(\pi_t-\bar{\pi})^2 + \frac{\alpha_W}{2}(\pi_t^w-\bar{\pi})^2 w_t
\end{equation}


\subsection{Government/Fiscal Authority}
\label{sec:govt}

The government issues one-period nominal bonds, purchases goods, pays lump-sum transfers to households, and collects tax revenue from all six tax instruments. The real government budget constraint is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gbc}
b_t = \frac{R_{t-1}\,b_{t-1}}{\pi_t} + g_t + x_t - taxrev_t
\end{equation}
where $b_t$ is real government debt, $g_t$ is real government purchases, $x_t$ is real lump-sum transfers, and $taxrev_t$ is total real tax revenue. Tax revenue is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taxrev}
\begin{split}
taxrev_t = &\;\tau_t^c c_t + \tau_t^w w_t h_t + \tau_t^k\!\left(r_t^k u_t k_{t-1} - \delta^{\tau}k_{t-1}^{\tau} - e_t^{\tau}i_t\right) \\
           & + \tau_t^i(R_{t-1}-1)\frac{b_{t-1}}{\pi_t} + \tau_t^d\,\text{div}_t - \tau_t^{ic}\,i_t
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The six components are: consumption tax revenue ($\tau_t^c c_t$), labor income tax revenue ($\tau_t^w w_t h_t$), capital income tax revenue net of tax depreciation deductions and immediate expensing ($\tau_t^k(\cdot)$), interest income tax revenue on bond interest ($\tau_t^i(R_{t-1}-1)b_{t-1}/\pi_t$), dividend tax revenue ($\tau_t^d\,\text{div}_t$), and investment tax credit outlays ($-\tau_t^{ic}\,i_t$).

\subsubsection*{Fiscal rules}

Each fiscal instrument follows an autoregressive rule that allows feedback from lagged government debt and lagged output, following \cn{Zubairy2010}. Debt feedback ensures intertemporal fiscal solvency; output feedback captures automatic stabilizers. Because $g_t$ and $x_t$ are expenditures that the government cuts when debt is elevated, their debt-feedback coefficients enter with a negative sign. Tax rates carry a positive debt-feedback sign, rising to close the fiscal gap.

Government purchases and lump-sum transfers follow:
\begin{align}
\log\!\left(\frac{g_t}{\bar{g}}\right) &= \rho_g\log\!\left(\frac{g_{t-1}}{\bar{g}}\right) - \rho_{g,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{g,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^g \label{eq:grule} \\
\log\!\left(\frac{x_t}{\bar{x}}\right) &= \rho_{x}\log\!\left(\frac{x_{t-1}}{\bar{x}}\right) - \rho_{x,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{x,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^{x} \label{eq:trrule}
\end{align}

The rules for the capital and labor income tax rates, which follow \cn{Zubairy2010}, are:
\begin{align}
\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_t^k}{\bar{\tau}^k}\right) &= \rho_k\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_{t-1}^k}{\bar{\tau}^k}\right) + \rho_{k,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{k,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^k \label{eq:taukrule} \\
\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_t^w}{\bar{\tau}^w}\right) &= \rho_w\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_{t-1}^w}{\bar{\tau}^w}\right) + \rho_{w,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{w,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \phi\,\varepsilon_t^k + \varepsilon_t^w \label{eq:taukwrule}
\end{align}
The term $\phi\,\varepsilon_t^k$ in the labor tax rule allows capital tax shocks to generate correlated movements in the labor tax rate, capturing the empirical co-movement between capital and labor income tax changes.

The four extension fiscal rules for the additional tax instruments take the same log-ratio form:
\begin{align}
\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_t^c}{\bar{\tau}^c}\right) &= \rho_c\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_{t-1}^c}{\bar{\tau}^c}\right) + \rho_{c,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{c,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^c \label{eq:taucrule} \\
\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_t^i}{\bar{\tau}^i}\right) &= \rho_i\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_{t-1}^i}{\bar{\tau}^i}\right) + \rho_{i,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{i,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^i \label{eq:tauirule} \\
\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_t^d}{\bar{\tau}^d}\right) &= \rho_d\log\!\left(\frac{\tau_{t-1}^d}{\bar{\tau}^d}\right) + \rho_{d,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{d,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \phi_{wd}\,\varepsilon_t^w + \varepsilon_t^d \label{eq:taudrule}
\end{align}
The dividend tax rule includes $\phi_{wd}\,\varepsilon_t^w$, capturing the empirical co-movement between changes in labor and dividend income tax rates. Because the steady-state values of the investment tax credit $\bar{\tau}^{ic}$ and the expensing rate $\bar{e}^{\tau}$ are calibrated to zero, their rules are specified in level-deviation form to avoid undefined log-ratios:
\begin{align}
\tau_t^{ic} - \bar{\tau}^{ic} &= \rho_{ic}(\tau_{t-1}^{ic} - \bar{\tau}^{ic}) + \rho_{ic,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{ic,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^{ic} \label{eq:tauicrule} \\
e_t^{\tau} - \bar{e}^{\tau} &= \rho_{e}(e_{t-1}^{\tau} - \bar{e}^{\tau}) + \rho_{e,b}\log\!\left(\frac{b_{t-1}}{\bar{b}}\right) + \rho_{e,y}\log\!\left(\frac{y_{t-1}}{\bar{y}}\right) + \varepsilon_t^{e} \label{eq:etaurule}
\end{align}



\subsection{Monetary Authority}

The monetary authority sets the gross nominal interest rate $R_t$ according to a Taylor rule with interest rate smoothing, following \cn{Zubairy2010}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:taylor}
\log\!\left(\frac{R_t}{\bar{R}}\right) = \alpha_R\log\!\left(\frac{R_{t-1}}{\bar{R}}\right) + (1-\alpha_R)\!\left[\alpha_\pi\log\!\left(\frac{\pi_t}{\bar{\pi}}\right) + \alpha_Y\log\!\left(\frac{y_t}{\bar{y}}\right)\right] + \varepsilon_t^m
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_R \in (0,1)$ governs interest rate inertia, $\alpha_\pi > 1$ is the response to inflation deviations from steady state (satisfying the Taylor principle), $\alpha_Y > 0$ is the response to output deviations, and $\varepsilon_t^m$ is a monetary policy shock. The interest income tax $\tau_t^i$ does not enter the Taylor rule directly; it affects the economy through the household bond Euler equation \eqref{eq:bondeuler}, which links the pre-tax policy rate $R_t$ set by the central bank to the after-tax return relevant for household saving decisions.

\subsection{Equilibrium}

\subsubsection{Market Clearing}

In a symmetric equilibrium all intermediate goods firms make identical factor choices, so labor and capital markets clear when household factor supply equals aggregate firm demand:
\begin{align}
h_t &= \int_0^1 h_{i,t}\,di \label{eq:labmc} \\
k_{t-1} &= \int_0^1 k_{i,t-1}\,di \label{eq:capmc}
\end{align}
In symmetric equilibrium these reduce to $h_{i,t} = h_t$ and $k_{i,t-1} = k_{t-1}$ for all $i \in [0,1]$.

The bond market clears when household bond holdings equal the government's outstanding debt. Since both the household budget constraint \eqref{eq:bc} and the government budget constraint \eqref{eq:gbc} use the same variable $b_t$, this condition holds by construction.

Goods market clearing requires total net output to equal total absorption. Combining the output definition \eqref{eq:ynet} with the resource constraint \eqref{eq:rc} yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:goodsmc}
y_t = c_t + g_t + i_t + \frac{\gamma_1}{1+\sigma_u}\!\left(u_t^{1+\sigma_u}-1\right)k_{t-1} + \frac{\alpha_W}{2}(\pi_t^w - \bar{\pi})^2 w_t
\end{equation}
Output $y_t$ is already net of the Rotemberg price adjustment cost (see equation \eqref{eq:ynet}), so only the utilization cost and wage adjustment cost appear on the right-hand side. By Walras' law, the bond market clearing condition is redundant given \eqref{eq:labmc}, \eqref{eq:capmc}, and \eqref{eq:goodsmc}.

These breakdown into the following groups:
\begin{itemize}
\item 4 choice variables (Household): $i,v,l,c$
\item 4 prices: $P,w,r,r^{k}$
\item 2 choice variables (gov't): $g,x$, reallly $2+n$ where $n$ is the number tax instruments
\item 1 choice variable (monetary authority): $M$
\item 2 exogenous state: $z,\varepsilon$
\item 5 endogenous state variable: $k,b,d,k^{\tau},Y$
\end{itemize}

Solve for these unknowns with the following equations:
\begin{itemize}
\item 5 FOCs, household $\implies i,v,l,c,b$
\item 3 from FOCs, intermediate goods producers $\implies r^{k},w,P$ (These FOC's give demand for effective capital and labor.  So together with the HH supply of these factors we can use our market clearing conditions to find the eq'm factor prices.)
\item 1 from the profit function of intermediate goods producers $\implies d$
\item 1 from FOCs, final goods producer $\implies Y$
\item 1 from the zero profit condition for the final goods producer $\implies P$ (This together with the FOC's from the intermediate goods producers gives you the aggregate price index.)
\item 1 Gov't budget constraint ($+n$ fiscal policy rules) $\implies X$, ($+n$ tax rates)
\item 1 Resource constraint $\implies G$
\item 2 laws of motion $\implies k^{\tau},k$
\item 3 exog shock process $\implies z,\varepsilon^{b},\lambda_{p}$
\item 1 Taylor rule for monetary authority $\implies r,M$ (There is the Taylor Rule plus the fact that $r=max(M,0)$ that determines both these.)
\end{itemize}
